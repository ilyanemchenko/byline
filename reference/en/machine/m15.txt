The first time I raised my rate, I lost a client.

The second time, I got three new ones.

Here's what changed between those two moments: I stopped pricing my hours and started pricing the problem.

When I charged $40/hour, every conversation was about time. How long will it take? Can you do it faster? Why did that revision take two hours?

When I started quoting per project, the conversation moved to outcomes. What's this worth to your business? What happens if it doesn't get fixed?

A few things I wish someone had told me earlier:

1. Your rate is a signal. Too low, and good clients wonder what's wrong. The ones who pick you for being cheap will leave for someone cheaper.

2. Hourly pricing punishes you for getting better. The faster you work, the less you earn. Fixed prices let your experience pay you.

3. Put a price on the scope, not the vibe. Write down exactly what's included. Everything else is a change request with its own number.

4. Raise rates for new clients first. Existing clients get notice and a date. Most stay. The ones who don't were making room for better work.

5. Say the number and stop talking. The silence after a quote feels awful. Filling it with discounts feels worse a month later.

The client I lost when I raised my rate? They came back eight months later. At the new price.

Pricing isn't math. It's deciding what your work is worth, then acting like you believe it.
