Most startups don't die because the idea was bad. They die because the founders ran out of time or money before they learned whether the idea was good.

The same patterns show up again and again:

1. Building before talking to customers. Months go into features nobody asked for. Ten real conversations with people who have the problem are worth more than ten months of code.

2. Confusing "nice to have" with "I'd pay for this." If people say "that's cool," that is not a signal. Ask for money, a pilot, or a date on the calendar.

3. Running out of cash before finding traction. The burn rate gets set by hope instead of by the revenue curve. Founders stretch the runway instead of cutting the plan.

4. Team problems. Unclear roles, equity disputes, or a co-founder who can no longer make hard calls. Many teams break on people long before they break on product.

5. Scaling too early. Hiring, paid marketing, and big infrastructure bets made for 10,000 users when 12 people actually use the product.

6. Ignoring unit economics. Growth that loses money on every customer is just a faster way to run out of cash.

7. Quitting the wrong thing. Some founders abandon the idea that was working because a new one feels more exciting. Others hold on to a dead idea out of pride.

The fix is rarely "work harder." It is shorter feedback loops:

- Talk to customers every week, and write down what they say before you pitch them anything.
- Frame each experiment around one question, such as "will 20 people pay $50 this month?"
- Know your runway in months, and make a decision well before it reaches zero.
- Decide in advance what evidence would make you stop or change direction.

The startups that survive are rarely the smartest. They are the ones that learn fastest and still have enough cash left to act on what they learn.
