Most one-on-ones I've sat through were status updates wearing a disguise.

The manager asks "what are you working on?", the report reads out their task list, and 30 minutes later both people know exactly what they already knew.

That meeting could have been a Slack message.

Here's what I've learned makes a one-on-one worth the calendar slot:

1. It's their meeting, not yours.
Let the other person own the agenda. Keep a shared doc where they add topics during the week. If it's empty, that's a signal too.

2. Skip the status update.
If you need project updates, get them somewhere else. Use this time for the things that never come up in standups: friction, frustration, ambition, confusion.

3. Ask questions that can't be answered with "fine."
"What's the most annoying part of your week?"
"What would you change about how our team works?"
"Is there anything you're doing that feels like a waste of your time?"

4. Listen more than you talk.
A rough rule: if you're speaking more than a third of the time, you're running a lecture.

5. Write down what you promised.
Nothing kills trust faster than "I'll look into that" followed by silence. Note every commitment and open the next one-on-one by closing the loop.

6. Don't cancel it.
Moving it is fine. Cancelling it repeatedly tells someone exactly where they rank on your list.

7. Talk about the long game sometimes.
Once a month, zoom out. Where do they want to be in a year? What skills do they want to build? What can you do to help?

The best one-on-ones don't feel like meetings. They feel like the one place at work where someone is fully paying attention to you.
