We launched to silence.

Months of building. A waitlist we were proud of. Launch day came, and the signups trickled in, then stopped. A week later, almost nobody had come back.

It stung. But looking back, the launch didn't fail on launch day. It failed long before, in decisions that felt reasonable at the time.

Here's what I took from it:

1. A waitlist is not demand.
People will give you an email address for free. Asking them to change how they work is a different request. I counted signups when I should have been counting conversations where someone described the problem in their own words, without me prompting it.

2. I built for the user in my head.
Every feature made sense to me. Very few of them came from watching a real person struggle with the problem. Now I don't write a line of a feature until I've seen someone hit the wall it's meant to remove.

3. "We'll fix it after launch" adds up.
Each shortcut was small. Together they made the first ten minutes confusing. People don't give a new product a second chance to explain itself.

4. Launch is a starting line, not a finish line.
I treated it as the big reveal. The products I admire treat launch as the moment the real learning begins: small, early, repeated.

5. Silence is data.
Nobody complained, so at first I assumed nothing was wrong. In fact, no complaints meant nobody cared enough to tell me. The fix was to go and ask, one person at a time.

The next version shipped smaller, earlier, to fewer people. It was less exciting. It worked better.

If you've had a launch that landed with a thud, what did it teach you?
