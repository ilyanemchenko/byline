For months, I did everything myself. Product, support, content, billing questions, and the late-night bug fixes. I told myself that hiring was for "later," when there was more revenue and more certainty.

Then I noticed the cost of waiting. Every week I spent on tasks that someone else could do better was a week I didn't spend on the things only I could do.

So I hired my first employee. Here is what I learned:

1. Hire for the problem, not the title. I didn't need a "Head of Operations." I needed someone to own onboarding, follow-ups, and the messy admin that was slowing us down. A clear problem makes the job description almost write itself.

2. Write down what you do before you hand it off. I thought I knew my own workflow. I didn't. Making a simple list of recurring tasks showed me where the real time went, and it became their first week's guide.

3. Expect to be slower for a while. Training takes time. The first month felt like I had more work, not less. That's normal. The payoff comes when they start making decisions without you.

4. Give ownership, not just tasks. The best early hires don't wait for instructions. Share the goal and the reason behind it, then let them find better ways to reach it.

5. Your first hire changes how you lead. I had to stop being the person who fixes everything and start being the person who sets direction and gives feedback. That was harder than any technical problem I've solved.

If you're a founder on the fence about your first hire, my advice is simple: don't wait until you feel ready. You'll never feel fully ready. Start with one clear problem, and be patient with the process.
