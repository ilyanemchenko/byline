Most feedback fails before the second sentence.

"Great job, but..." tells the person to ignore the first half and brace for the second.
"You need to be more strategic" tells them nothing they can act on.

Here is what I've seen work after years of giving and receiving feedback:

1. Describe what happened, not who they are.
"In the meeting you interrupted Sam twice" is something a person can fix. "You're not a good listener" is a verdict, and people defend against verdicts.

2. Say the effect it had.
"After that, Sam stopped sharing ideas" gives the behavior a reason to change. Without the effect, it sounds like a matter of your taste.

3. Give it fast.
Feedback a month old feels like a case file. Feedback from the same day feels like help.

4. Make it private for the hard parts and public for the good parts.
Praise that is specific ("your summary saved us an hour of debate") teaches as much as criticism does.

5. Ask before you tell.
"How do you think the presentation went?" often gets you to the same point, and the person owns the conclusion instead of resisting yours.

6. End with a next step.
Not "do better," but "next time, send the draft a day earlier so we can review it together."

The test I use: could the person replay your feedback on video and agree it happened? If yes, it is probably useful. If it needs a personality assessment, rewrite it.
